1. Talk to it, don't type

When you type, you edit before you finish thinking. What lands in the box is the compressed version, and that compressed version is all the AI ever sees. When you talk, you give it the raw material: the side thoughts, the uncertainty, the bits you would have deleted. That is exactly what makes the output useful.

The comparison worth holding onto: explaining a problem to a mate by text message, versus sitting down with them for ten minutes and rambling it out over a coffee.

Talk it out. When you start something new, don't hunt for the perfect prompt. Tell the AI what you're trying to do, ramble, let it interview you, and let the brief come out of that conversation. You aren't really talking to the AI. You're talking to yourself, and it's organising your thinking.

2. Give it a role worth having

Ask for a spaghetti bolognese recipe and it reads the whole internet and hands you the average of everything. Ask it to act as an Italian chef and you've narrowed it. Ask it to act as a chef from a specific region, with a specific style and a specific number of years behind them, and you've summoned a specialist.

"Act as a buyer's agent" is the generic version. It's the equivalent of "act as a general manager." Go further: the region you buy in, the client type you deal with, the years of experience, the thing you're known for.

Go and research who is considered the most respected buyer's agent working in [your area]. Then act as that person for the rest of this conversation.

Stop it agreeing with you

The AI's default is to validate whatever you put in front of it. Ask whether you could mix peanut butter through a bolognese and it will find a reason why that could work. Useful for confidence, useless for decisions.

Red team this. Give me every reason this plan would fail.

3. Projects

A project is a container that holds your business context, so you stop introducing yourself every time you open a chat. Starting fresh every time is exactly why results feel inconsistent.

Most people who say they use projects have a folder with some files in it. That's a start, but it isn't the part that does the work. The instructions are.

Instructions and files do different jobs

The instructions tell it how to behave. The files you upload are reference material it draws on. Don't put your whole business into the instructions box, and don't expect a folder of PDFs to change how it writes.

What goes in the instructions

  • Who you are. Your business, your patch, your client type.
  • What you sell. Services, pricing, the shape of your offer.
  • How you write. Your tone, and the things you'd never say.
  • What to avoid. Often clearer than telling it what to do.

What makes instructions actually work

  • Specific beats general. "Be professional" changes nothing, it's already trying. "This client wants the reasoning behind every recommendation, not just the answer" changes the output.
  • One good example beats ten rules. If you've got a summary or an email you were happy with, put it in. It'll match that standard better than any description of it.
  • Cut anything that wouldn't change the output. Instructions get read into every conversation in that project, so padding costs you.

Two things worth knowing

  • Project instructions override your global personal instructions. Inside that project, the project wins.
  • Projects can't talk to each other. If you want context moved across, ask one project to write a brief and paste it into the other.

Building one properly

Don't write the instructions from a blank page. Get it to interview you first.

I'm a buyer's agent and I want you to interview me so we can build a project brief for handling my client discovery calls. Ask me ONE question at a time. Wait for my answer. Then ask the next one. Do not list the questions or ask two at once. Cover: what kind of clients I work with, where I buy and where my clients are based, what I ask on a typical discovery call, what I'm trying to find out by the end of it, the one thing that would cause a problem later if I missed it, how I work out budget and finance readiness, how I read whether someone is ready to move, whether my clients want hand-holding or straight talk, and what I commit to right after the call. Once you have all the answers, write it up as project instructions.

Then paste the result into Project ‚Üí Instructions and save it. That's the setup. Half an hour, once.

4. Skills

A skill is a repeatable workflow written down like a recipe. You write it once, save it, and invoke it whenever you need that job done. If a project is the workspace, a skill is the specialist you bring into it.

This is the better answer to "should I have a project for each role in my business." Don't build a project per virtual employee. Build one project for the area of work, and let skills be the specialists you call in. You can invoke several at once.

The discovery call skill

Give it a transcript and it returns four things in one pass:

  • A structured client brief, budget through to decision-making style
  • A follow-up email drafted in your voice
  • Your action list
  • What you missed, questions you should have asked, vague answers worth chasing, anyone who wasn't on the call who should have been

That fourth one is usually the one that earns its keep.

Ask it for more off the same call

Once it's produced those four, it offers a menu. A calendar invite for the next step, a condensed note to paste into your CRM, a search brief you can hand to someone else, or presentation content.

On that last one, be realistic about when it's useful. A deck summarising a discovery call is over-engineered, your client had that conversation an hour ago and the email does the job better. Where a presentation earns its place is the shortlist stage, when you've got three properties, the comparables and a recommendation.

The menu is just something written into the skill. Whatever your process actually is, that's what goes in it.

Treat it like a recipe

It won't be right first time. When the output isn't what you want, say so in plain words. "Never open an email like that." "Don't use em dashes." "Say it the way I'd say it." It edits itself. Every correction sticks.

5. Cowork

Chat is where you think. Cowork is where things get done. Cowork can reach your files, your drive, your email and the web, and work through a multi-step job on its own.

Cowork needs the desktop app. The browser version can't reach your computer.

How to run it

  • Point it at a specific folder. That's where it works from.
  • Give it the goal, ask for the plan, then approve it. Don't hand over the keys and walk away.
  • Keep it on manual approve while you're learning what it does.
Ask someone to tidy your kitchen with no instructions and they'll do a beautiful job. You just won't be able to find anything afterwards. Same principle.

Scheduled tasks

Anything you do on a rhythm can run on a schedule. A daily email triage that reads your inbox at five o'clock and comes back red, amber, green. A weekly newsletter built from the week's material. Monthly reminders.

6. Connectors

Settings ‚Üí Connectors

Connectors let Claude work with your other tools directly. Start with email and calendar, that's where most of the value is.

  • Gmail and Outlook. Read, draft, send. Outlook takes a bit more setup than Gmail.
  • Google Calendar. Check availability, create invites.
  • Fireflies and similar. Pull a transcript straight in without copying and pasting.
  • CRMs. HubSpot, Zoho and others connect directly. High Level now has an MCP you can set up manually.

Once your transcript tool is connected, you can skip the paste entirely:

Go to my last transcript with [client name] and run the discovery call processor. Draft the follow-up email, give me the action points, and tell me whether I should be booking a calendar invite.

Transcripts

Zoom and Google Meet both generate transcripts natively. Fireflies and Otter both work well if you want something dedicated. Any of them will do, this all runs off calls you're already having.

7. Privacy

Do this now

Settings ‚Üí Privacy ‚Üí Help improve our AI models and switch it off. Takes ten seconds. On a free account this is on by default.

Rules of thumb

  • No passports, no licences, no bank statements, no card details.
  • Don't upload someone's personal information without their say-so.
  • Tell people you're recording. Same as any call.
  • Strip names and addresses out of a transcript before pasting it. It works just as well.
  • Only put in what the job needs. Convenience isn't a reason to hand over more.

A transcript holds more than it looks like

It doesn't feel like a document, so it doesn't get treated like one. But a discovery call transcript has your client's budget, their borrowing capacity, their family circumstances, and sometimes health or relationship detail they mentioned in passing. That's personal information, same as anything else you'd handle carefully.

When a client asks whether their information trains the AI

They will eventually, and "I think it's fine" isn't an answer. Know your setting, know your plan. A paid account handles this differently to a free one, and a Team or business plan differently again. If you're running client material through it regularly, the paid tier is worth the difference for that reason alone.

Cowork and connected accounts

The risk isn't that the AI does something malicious. It's that broad access to your inbox means broad access to whatever's sitting in your inbox, including client documents you'd forgotten were there.

  • Keep sensitive material in a folder you don't grant access to
  • Point it at a specific folder rather than everything
  • Manual approve while you build trust in it

Worth checking with your own compliance people

Buyer's agents came under the AML tranche two reforms from 1 July this year. That changes what identity data you hold and how it has to be handled, and it's worth confirming what applies to you. There's also a requirement coming in December around disclosing AI use to clients. Neither of these is AI-specific advice, it's your industry's rules, so check them properly rather than taking my word for it.

8. Which model to use

  • Everyday work. Sonnet, standard effort. Drafting, summarising, questions.
  • Deep work. Higher model for research, building something, reorganising a lot of files.
  • Running the biggest model on high effort for a simple question wastes minutes and burns through your usage for no benefit.
This is the job I'm about to do. Which model would you recommend, and be honest rather than pushing me to the biggest one.

Ship it

The AI will always offer you one more improvement. Ask it to critique your work fifty times and it will find something fifty times. A ten minute job becomes an hour. Get it good and send it.

9. Turn it on yourself

Everything above points the AI at your client. The other direction is more uncomfortable and probably more useful.

The same transcript that produces a client brief can tell you how you performed. Ask it after a call and it will tell you things you'd rather not hear. What percentage of that meeting you spoke for. Which questions you didn't ask. Where you talked past a buying signal. Whether you were selling when you should have been listening.

Review my performance on that call. What percentage of it did I speak? What questions should I have asked and didn't? Where did I miss something the client said? Be direct, I'm not looking for encouragement.

That last line matters. Left alone it'll tell you that you did great, because its instinct is to make you feel good. You have to ask for the honest version.

Discovery is listening, gathering intelligence, building rapport, finding the real problem. If you leave a call and can't remember what they said because you were busy talking, the transcript will tell you exactly that.

10. Where to start this week

  • Turn the privacy setting off. Ten seconds.
  • Install Wispr Flow and use voice for a week. This one changes the most.
  • Build one project using the interview prompt above. Half an hour.
  • Connect your email and calendar.
  • Run one transcript through the discovery call skill and see what it catches.
  • Ask it to review one of your own calls. Brace yourself.
The tools change every week. The strategy doesn't. Your job isn't to do the work the AI does, it's to brief it properly and sign off on what comes back. It can write the recipe. It can't taste the food.
