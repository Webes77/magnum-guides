Cowork, set up properly

A chat answers you. Cowork does the job. It is the same Claude you already pay for, in a mode where you hand over a task and get back the finished thing instead of a reply. This page is what it is, which one to open, the setup in six steps, and two prompts to run the day it is on.

What Cowork is

A second mode inside the Claude you already pay for

There is nothing to buy. Cowork comes with Pro, Max, Team and Enterprise, and if you are reading this you are on one of them. If the question is which plan your staff should be on rather than whether you have Cowork, that is a separate guide.

The difference between a chat and Cowork is the difference between asking someone a question and giving them a job. In a chat you send a message and read the answer, then send the next one. You are doing the driving. In Cowork you describe the outcome you want, Claude works out the steps, then goes and does them: reading your files, writing new ones, running code, looking things up. It comes back when the work is finished.

You describe the end

Not the steps. “Sort out my desktop and tell me what you moved” is the whole instruction. Working out the order is its job, not yours.

It works on its own

Many steps, one after another, without you approving each one. You set how often it stops to check with you, and that setting is yours to tighten.

You get the thing

A tidied folder, a spreadsheet, a written report, a file on your disk. Not a description of how you might do it.

The one-line versionA chat gives you an answer. Cowork gives you the work.

What carries across, and what does not

This is the part people get wrong in both directions, so it is worth being exact.

Memory carries. Since 25 August 2026, what Claude remembers about you and your business is one store shared by chat and Cowork. Something you established in a chat is there in a Cowork task, and something that came up in a Cowork task is there next time you chat. You can read the lot under Settings › Memory, where it is listed by topic, and delete anything you would rather it forgot.

The conversation does not. A new Cowork task does not go and read yesterday's chat thread. So anything a task needs in order to be done properly has to be in the task itself, in the folder you gave it, or in your instructions. That is why the setup below is worth the fifteen minutes.

Desktop or web

Open the desktop app

That is the answer for almost everybody, for one reason: your files are on your computer, and the desktop app is what reaches them.

Cowork runs on desktop for Mac and Windows, on the web, and on the phone app. The work itself now happens on Anthropic's servers rather than on your machine, which is why a task keeps running after you close the lid and why a session you start at your desk is there on your phone. What the desktop app adds is the bridge to your own files and the built-in browser.

Web and mobile have been rolling out since 7 July 2026, starting with Max and working down the plans, and they are still in beta. Use them the way you would use a phone: to check on a task, read what came back, or start something while you are away from your desk. Do the setup on the desktop.

Setting it up

Six steps, about fifteen minutes, done once

  1. Open Cowork

    Open the Claude desktop app and sign in. In the message box, switch from Chat to Cowork. Check 01

    You are in the right place when the box is asking you for a task rather than a message.

  2. Give it one folder

    Cowork does not create a folder for itself and there is no default location. You choose an existing folder and grant access to it, either in the settings or when Claude asks for it partway through a job. Check 02

    Start with one folder, not your whole drive. A project folder, or Desktop if the first thing you want is the desktop cleared. You can add more later, and the smaller the first grant, the easier it is to see what it did.

    Worth knowingGranting a folder is not the same as uploading a file. Nothing is copied anywhere. Claude reads and changes the files where they already sit, which is the whole point, and also the reason the first prompt below never deletes anything. Check 03

  3. Set how often it checks with you

    Cowork asks before it uses a connected tool, and you control how often. The choice is between asking you every time, asking once and then carrying on, or not asking at all.

    Start on the setting that asks every time. Once you have watched it work through two or three jobs and you trust what it reaches for, loosen it. Going the other way, tightening after something surprising, is a worse way to learn.

  4. Connect Gmail, Drive and Calendar once

    Settings › Connectors

    Connectors are not a separate Cowork setting. You authorise Gmail, Google Drive or Calendar once, in the same place you would for chat, and they are available in both. If you already connected them for your everyday chats, they are already on here and there is nothing to do at this step.

  5. Turn on skill creation

    Settings › Capabilities › Skills

    A skill is a job description you write once and Claude loads whenever it applies. Switch on the skill creator, and from then on you can describe a skill in an ordinary conversation and have Claude write it for you. The second prompt below ends by producing one. Check 05

  6. Schedule something, when you have a job worth repeating

    Type /schedule inside a Cowork task, or open Scheduled in the left sidebar to see and manage the ones you have. Check 06

    Scheduled tasks run on Anthropic's servers, so your computer does not need to be awake or even on. Leave this one until you have a job you have run by hand a few times and want repeated. A schedule is a good way to automate a job you have already got right, and a fast way to repeat one you have not.

Two prompts to run first

One proves it works on your files. One gets it writing like you.

Both are on the Prompt Shelf under Setting up, so you can find them again without coming back here. Paste each into a new Cowork task.

Prompt one · run it in a Cowork task with your Desktop folder granted

Clear the desktop

The first job worth giving it, because the result is visible in ten minutes and it teaches you what folder access actually means. It never deletes anything. Every file it touches goes into one dated review folder with a written record of where each one came from, and you empty that folder yourself when you are satisfied.

You are a careful assistant sorting out my computer desktop. Nothing on it
is yours to throw away, so this job is to sort and move, never to delete.

Work only in the folder I have given you access to.

The rules, and why each one is there:
- Move files, never delete them. I am the only one who knows which of these
  still matter, so the deciding is mine to do.
- Put everything you move into one folder named Review, followed by today's
  date, so nothing is lost while I work through it.
- Leave anything you cannot identify exactly where it is, and list it for
  me. A guess about where a file belongs is worse than leaving it alone.
- Check with me before moving anything that looks like it is in use: a file
  changed today, or a lock file.

Work through this in order:
1. List what is on the desktop. Group it by what it is: documents,
   images, screenshots, installers, folders, and anything else.
2. Show me the groups, tell me how many files are in each, and say where
   you propose to put each group. Then wait for me to say go.
3. Once I say go, create the folders and move the files.
4. Write a file named what-moved.md inside the review folder, listing every
   file, where it came from and where it went, so I can undo any single
   move by hand.

Stop after step 4. Tell me what you left behind and why.

After it runs

  1. Open what-moved.md in the review folder before you open anything else. It is the list of every move, and it is what makes each one reversible.
  2. Work through the review folder yourself. Nothing has been deleted, so there is no hurry and no risk in taking a week over it.
  3. If it left files behind and told you why, that is the prompt working. Files in use and files it could not identify are meant to stay put.

Prompt two · run it in a Cowork task with Gmail connected

Learn how I write, from my sent mail

The complaint about AI writing is that it does not sound like you. This is the fix, and it works because the evidence already exists: a few hundred emails you wrote yourself. The prompt reads your sent mail, tells you what it found, asks you what to keep, and turns the answer into a skill that loads every time you ask for something written.

You are a writing analyst working out how I actually write, so that Claude
can draft in my voice instead of its own.

Read my sent mail only. What I received is other people's writing, and it
would teach you the wrong voice.

Count an email as mine when I wrote it from scratch, it runs to more than
about eighty words, and it went to a real person. Skip forwards, one-line
replies, automated mail, and anything I pasted in from somewhere else,
because none of it shows how I write.

Work through this in order:
1. Read at least thirty of my sent emails from the last twelve months.
2. Tell me what you found, under these headings:
   - Sentence length and rhythm, with two real examples.
   - Words and phrases I use often, and ones I never use.
   - How I open, and how I sign off.
   - How formal I am, and whether that changes with who I am writing to.
   - What I do that most people do not.
3. Mark any point you are drawing from fewer than five emails as
   [NEEDS CONFIRMING], so I can tell a real pattern from a one-off.
4. Ask me which of it to keep, and which is a habit I would rather drop.
   Ask one question at a time and wait for my answer.
5. Write the result as a skill: a description saying when it should fire,
   then the rules for writing as me, each rule with its reason.

Stop after step 5 and show me the skill. I will save it myself.

After it runs

  1. Read what it says about your writing before you agree to any of it. Some of what it finds will be your voice and some will be a habit you have been meaning to drop, and only you can tell which is which.
  2. Anything marked [NEEDS CONFIRMING] came from too few examples to trust. Confirm it or cut it.
  3. Save the skill yourself once you are happy with it. Then test it: ask for an email in a new session and see whether it sounds like you.
