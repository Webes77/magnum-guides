This week

Why the typed brief is always thin.

I watched a client type a brief last week. Three lines. It took him four minutes. Then he told me what he actually wanted, out loud, in about forty seconds, and it was three times as much information.

That is the whole Field Note. When you type, your brain turns into an editor. You shorten, you tidy, you leave things out, and you try to make it sound right before you have finished thinking. What lands in the box is a compressed version of what is in your head.

When you talk, you hand over the raw material. The side thought, the example, the bit you are not sure about, the throwaway line about why the last one went wrong. That is the stuff the AI needs to work out what you mean, not just what you wrote.

I did this wrong for a long time. I typed like I was sending a text and then wondered why the answer read like a template.

If you had a problem with a job, would you text your business partner two lines about it, or talk it through over a coffee? The coffee gets to the answer faster every time. This is the same thing.

Three times the words. A tenth of the effort.

Editor and raw material

Typing edits. Talking thinks out loud.

Both are you. They hand the AI very different things.

What typing gives it

The finished sentence. Tidy, short, and missing everything you decided was not worth the effort of typing.

Your fingers run at 30 to 50 words a minute, so your brain rations what gets through.

The conclusion
The polite version
Whatever fit in the box
What talking gives it

The thinking. The example you would have cut. The doubt. The reason the last attempt failed. The bit you only worked out as you said it.

You speak at 120 to 150 words a minute. Nothing gets rationed.

Context
Examples
Uncertainty
What wrong looks like
The
trap

Treating dictation as a faster keyboard. People turn it on, then dictate the same tidy two lines they would have typed, only quicker. Nothing changes.

Dictation is not about speed. It is about letting the AI hear the train of thought instead of the final sentence. Ramble. That is the point.

You think you are talking to the AI.
You are talking to yourself. It is taking notes.

The deliverable

Five rules for talking to it.

None of these need practice. They need permission.

01

Say it rough.

Do not compose. Say it the way you would say it to someone in the ute. Half sentences are fine. It tidies. That is its job, not yours.

02

Say why.

The reason behind the ask is the most useful thing you own, and it is the first thing typing cuts. “Because the last one sounded too formal” changes the whole answer.

03

Say what wrong looks like.

You know what you do not want long before you know what you want. Say it out loud. “Not salesy, not long, not another one of those templated ones.”

04

Let it ask.

Tell it to interview you, one question at a time. The questions pull out what you forgot you knew.

05

Read before you send.

The one place to slow down. Talking gets you a better draft. It is not yours until you have read it.

Rough in, tidy out.
You supply the thinking. It supplies the sentences.

Prompt to steal

Talk it through, one question at a time.

This is the Talk-It-Out prompt from session one. Paste it, then say the job out loud where the last line asks for it.

Prompt / Talk-It-Out 
I want your help with a real task, and I would rather talk it through than write you a brief.

Interview me. Ask one question at a time and wait for my answer before the next one. Work out what I actually need, who it is for, what it has to do, and what would make it wrong.

Do not start producing anything until you have enough. When you do, tell me what you understood before you hand me the result.

The task is: [say it roughly, out loud, in your own words]
Tip. Put the mic on before you paste. Whatever it asks first, answer out loud.
Use it like this
01

Turn dictation on. Mac: press fn twice. Windows: the Windows key and H. Phone: the microphone in the message box.

02

Paste the prompt. Where it says the task is, say the job roughly.

03

Answer each question out loud. Rough is fine. Read the result before you use it.

Best for

Anything you would normally overthink the brief for. An email that matters, a proposal, a plan, a difficult message.

Not ideal for

A one-line factual question. Ask it straight and move on.

Make it automatic

Mic on. Say the mess. Read the result.

Three habits. Two are settings, one is discipline.

01

Set the shortcut once

Mac: fn twice. Windows: the Windows key and H. Phone: the microphone in the message box. Learn one. Use it every time.

Set once
02

Open with the mess

Start every real task by saying it rough, with the why and the not-this. Thirty seconds. Then let it ask.

Every task
03

Read before it leaves

Nothing goes out unread. Talking makes the draft better. Reading makes it yours.

Every time
Dictation is not a faster keyboard.
It is the AI in the passenger seat while you think.
It cannot read your mind. This is the closest thing to it.

Run it yourself

Five exercises. Ninety seconds to ten minutes.

Do not take my word for it. Watch the difference on your own screen.

01

Same job, twice

  1. Type a brief for a real email, the way you normally would. Get the draft.
  2. New chat. Say the same job out loud, rough, with the why.
  3. Put the two drafts side by side.
What you learn. The spoken one is longer and closer. Do this one first.
02

Count your words

  1. Say a job out loud for thirty seconds.
  2. Look at how much landed in the box.
  3. Now type the same thing and time it.
What you learn. About 70 words spoken. You would have typed 20 and cut the useful bits.
03

Say the not-this

  1. Pick a task you have been unhappy with the results on.
  2. Say only what wrong looks like. Three things.
  3. Then ask for it.
What you learn. Naming the wrong version fixes more than describing the right one.
04

Let it interview you

  1. Paste the page 05 prompt.
  2. Say the task in one rough sentence.
  3. Answer every question out loud.
What you learn. The third question is usually the one you had not thought about.
05

Walk and talk

  1. Open the phone app.
  2. Say the plan for tomorrow while you walk.
  3. Ask it for the three things that actually matter.
What you learn. You think better moving. The phone is the best dictation device you own.
