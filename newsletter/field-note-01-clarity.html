I’m away at the moment, so this Field Note is in place of the usual weekly newsletter.

I’ve been looking at ASD-STE100, a writing standard originally designed to make technical information easier to understand.

You do not need to become a technical writer to get value from it. A few of the principles are genuinely useful when working with AI.

They help remove vague language, tighten instructions and make outputs easier to act on.

That matters most when you are creating SOPs, checklists, handovers, training notes or anything another person needs to follow clearly.

In this Field Note, I’ve pulled out the parts I think are most useful, given you prompts you can copy straight into ChatGPT or Claude, and shown you how to make the behaviour more consistent inside your AI setup.

Why it matters

Clear writing saves more time than clever writing.

Most AI writing problems are not creativity problems. They are clarity problems.

When instructions are vague, people hesitate, interpret and make avoidable errors.

Less interpretation

The reader should not have to guess what you mean.

Faster execution

Clear steps reduce hesitation and speed up action.

Better handovers

Consistent language makes work easier to follow across a team.

Vague
“soon”
By 4pm Friday
Vague
“as required”
When the count drops below ten
Vague
“regularly”
Every Monday morning
If the reader has to stop and think,
the writing still has work to do.

The deliverable

Five rules worth stealing.

You do not need strict aerospace compliance. Use the parts that make writing easier to act on.

01

One idea per sentence.

Long sentences hide weak thinking.

02

Use one term for one thing.

Do not rename the same item halfway through.

03

Prefer active voice.

“Send the quote” beats “The quote should be sent.”

04

Put actions in order.

Write the steps in the sequence they happen.

05

Remove vague wording.

Replace “soon” and “as required” with something specific.

The goal is not robotic writing.
The goal is to make the reader work less.
