This week

Why every answer comes back too long.

Last month I asked it for ideas on a price rise letter and got eleven hundred words back. Good words. I read them twice and did nothing, because nothing in there told me what to do.

That is the whole Field Note. It has read everything, so when you ask for more, it gives you everything. Ten ideas when you needed one. A page when you needed a paragraph. That is not the model being generous. It is the model with no instruction to stop.

It is like asking a builder for options on a kitchen with no budget and no brief. He will happily draw you six. What you needed was one you could sign.

I did this for a year. Longer prompts, longer answers, more tabs open, fewer decisions. The fix turned out to be the opposite of what I was doing. Stop asking it to add. Ask it to cut.

In any answer, about a fifth of it does the work. The rest is context, hedging, and the model showing its working. Your job is not to read the pile. Your job is to make it find the fifth.

More options make a bigger pile. Fewer make a decision.

Adding and cutting

Ask for more, get a pile. Ask it to cut, get a decision.

Same tool. One of them makes work for you.

What adding gives you

Everything it knows about the subject, in the order it has seen it most. Ten ideas when you needed one. A page when you needed a paragraph.

You do the sorting. Which was the job you were trying to hand over.

Options
Context
Hedging
Its working
What cutting gives you

The fifth that does the work. Three points, not ten. The next action.

And what to ignore, which is the bit nobody asks for and the bit that saves the most time.

The core insight
Three points
One action
What to ignore
The
trap

Asking it to summarise. A summary is the same pile, shorter. It keeps everything in proportion, so the weak bits survive in miniature.

Cutting is different. You are telling it to throw the weak four fifths away and show you what it threw. The list of what went is the check.

It is not being thorough.
It has not been told where to stop.

The deliverable

Five rules for cutting.

None of them need a new tool. They need a different ask.

01

Ask for the fifth.

Not “summarise”. Not “shorten”. “Cut this to the 20 per cent that does the work.” The number matters, because it forces a choice instead of a trim.

02

Nothing new.

Tell it to cut and it will want to add. Say it plainly: nothing new, nothing rewritten, only what was there, minus the weak bits. Otherwise you get a second pile.

03

Make it say what went.

The list of what it dropped is your check. If it binned the one thing you cared about, you see it in ten seconds and put it back.

04

End on a decision.

Every session finishes with one action or one decision. If it does not, you got a reading list, not an answer.

05

Cut your own drafts too.

Paste your email in and ask for the fifth. It works on you as well as it works on itself, and you will not enjoy what it finds.

Prompt to steal

Cut it to the 20 per cent.

This is the card from the Prompt Shelf. Paste the long answer, or your own draft, where the first line asks for it.

Prompt / Cut it to the 20 per cent 
[paste the writing]

That is a piece of my writing. Make it better by removing, not adding.

Cut the weakest 20 per cent: the sentences that repeat, hedge, warm up, or explain what the reader already knows. Keep my wording for everything that survives, because the point is my writing with less in it, not yours.

Answer in this exact format:
THE CUT VERSION: the full text, a fifth shorter, in my words.
WHAT WENT: each line you removed, with a phrase on why.

Stop after that list.
Tip. Run it on the answer you got, not the question you asked. It works on output. Its own or yours.
Use it like this
01

Take the last long answer it gave you. Copy the whole thing.

02

New message. Paste it at the top, then the prompt underneath.

03

Read what went before you read what stayed. That list is the check.

Best for

Research answers, plans, lists of ideas, anything that came back longer than a screen. Your own emails and proposals.

Not ideal for

A checklist you need in full. A contract. Anything where the other four fifths are the point.

Make it automatic

Ask. Cut. Decide.

Three habits. One is a prompt, two are discipline.

01

Keep the cut one paste away

Save the page 05 prompt where you keep the others. Every long answer gets it before you read the whole thing.

Set once
02

Say the number first

“Three options.” “Under 100 words.” “One action.” It fills whatever space you give it, so give it less.

Every task
03

Close with a decision

Before you leave the chat, ask what you do first. If you cannot say it in one line, you are not finished.

Every time
More is not thorough.
More is the model with nowhere to stop.
Give it a fifth to find. It finds it every time.

Run it yourself

Five exercises. Two minutes to ten.

Do not take my word for it. Watch the pile shrink on your own screen.

01

Cut the last answer

  1. Find the last long answer it gave you.
  2. Paste it and run the page 05 prompt.
  3. Read WHAT WENT first.
What you learn. Nothing you needed is in the bin. Do this one first.
02

Ten, then three

  1. Ask for ten ideas on a real problem.
  2. In the same chat, ask for the three that would move it, and why.
  3. Look at which three it picked.
What you learn. You would have picked the same three. It took a page to get there.
03

Cut your own email

  1. Paste an email you are about to send.
  2. Ask for the 20 per cent.
  3. Send the cut one.
What you learn. Nobody notices what went. They notice they replied faster.
04

Say the number first

  1. Ask a question the way you normally would.
  2. New chat. Same question, plus “under 80 words, one recommendation”.
  3. Put them side by side.
What you learn. The second one is the one you use.
05

The one-line close

  1. At the end of any chat today, ask “what do I do first?”
  2. If the answer is longer than one line, ask again.
  3. Do it.
What you learn. A session that ends in an action is worth ten that end in a reading list.
