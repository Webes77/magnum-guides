Weekly Field Note / No. 05 / Arguing

It will tell you the plan is good.
It has not checked.

One prompt. Ninety seconds. It finds the hole before your client does.

What it is for
Finding the hole in a plan before you act on it, instead of after.
Why you would use it
It is built to be helpful, so it tells you the plan is good. A confident answer and a checked one read exactly the same.
How it works
Paste this straight after it hands you a plan. Then fix what it finds instead of defending the plan back to it.
The prompt
Before I act on this, take the other side.

Argue against it. Tell me where the logic is weakest, what I have assumed without checking, and what could go wrong that I have not accounted for.

Then tell me the things I need to know about this that I have not asked you.

This week

A confident answer and a checked answer look identical.

I built a cost calculator for this members area. Staff count in, seat cost out. I asked the model whether it was the right tool and it said yes, with four reasons, so I shipped it. Two days later I looked properly. It was answering a question nobody asks, because both platforms charge the same twenty dollars a seat. The cost was never the decision.

It is like asking a builder whether the job is fine. He says it is fine. He is not lying to you, he is answering the question you asked. Ask him what he would pull up if it were his own house and you get a completely different conversation.

I took plans at face value for the best part of a year, because the answers were good enough that checking them felt like doubting myself. Now nothing gets acted on until I have made it pull the plan apart, and that takes ninety seconds.

Agreeing tells you what you want to hear. Arguing tells you what you need to fix.

The deliverable

Five rules for arguing with it.

None of them need a new tool. They need you to change one word.

01

Argue, don't agree.

Do not ask "does this work". Say "argue against this" instead. An assistant defaults to agreeing with whatever you put in front of it, so you have to ask it not to.

02

Name your assumptions.

Get it to list what the plan depends on before it argues the case. A weak assumption named early is cheap to fix and expensive to find later.

03

Let it ask back.

Read what it says it was not told, not only what it disagrees with. A gap in your own brief is a second finding, not a failure of the plan.

04

Argue before it ships.

Run it on the plan, the quote or the email before a client sees it, not after. The argument is worth nothing once the thing is already out the door.

05

Fix it. Don't defend it.

Change what it finds instead of arguing back at it. The point of the exercise is a better plan, not a plan you talked it out of doubting.

Agreement is comfortable.
Arguing is cheaper.
